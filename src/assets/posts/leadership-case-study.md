I have been having conversations with several team leaders of technical teams, exploring the leadership aspects of the job. As part of a mentorship program at my company (awesome idea), I have been a mentee for quite a while now and I want to compile the intelligence extracted from a series of very interesting conversations.

There are a lot of instances documented where the same team can thrive or fail just by changing leadership. This is a phenomenon that has always fascinated me.

There is a lot of literature about leadership and different ways to lead a team of people, each one of them claims to be the definitive one. The leadership style must match the circumstances of the team, the nature of the endeavor and the temperament of the team members.

> _Leadership is an obligation, taking the responsibility through the good times and the bad. If the job is easy, you probably are doing it wrong._

# Why should someone do as the leader says?

The reality is that grow ups don’t like to be told what to do. And nor should anyone do it. So, usually, _telling people what to do because you are the leader_ it is not a good strategy.

If the team trust the leader, then the leader can be dedicated to motivate the team and influence other how to follow you. There are a lot of styles, here a few examples:

1. Charisma. Capturing imagination of people with your words
2. Describing what the problem is but not describing how to get there
3. Engaging them to be the best professional they can
4. Creating a **clear** mission statement, vision, manifesto or mandate

The leader should have a vision and a plan to implement that vision. Then, why someone should trust this plan? Well, nobody should trust your plan "just because", you have to create and establish **trust**. How do you do that?

# Establishing trust

Trust is the assured reliance on the character, ability, strength, or truth of someone. When you trust someone, you feel like you can place confidence on that person. Trust has many benefits but has a big problem: it is very difficult to establish and easy to lose. It will take you a long time to make people trust you but just a few minutes to ruin it. Trust is expensive, so treat it accordingly.

One way to make people trust you as a leader is being generous in delegating because trust is a two-way street. You should delegate and trust the job will be done. That empowers people to own the task and pursuit it with all consequences. You should respect other people ideas and give positive reinforcement.

Using diplomacy is also a good way to earn trust; you don't want to favor one person over the other, so you need to deal with people in a sensitive and tactful way. When rejecting an idea, you should use formulas like _it's a good idea but still we are going to go with..._ it shows respect and appreciation for the contribution, but still is assertive enough. You should also take this into account when giving positive feedback, you want to choose your words very carefully.

You don’t want to do **secret management** were nobody knows about the process, the leadership style or what the direction is. The process should be open and understood. Rather, a leader **manages secrets**, which is totally different. A leader may have information about personal circumstances, personal agreements and conditions, private conversations with other managers, promotions and organizational changes, teams' reorganization... So, we don’t want to do secret management, but manage secrets.

# Are consensus decisions at odds with smart decisions?

Some managers rely on consensus to make some decisions to avoid conflict and create a more participative environment. Some others think that consensus decisions overturn smart decisions. Consensus is a general agreement, so it looks like the perfect solution, what is the problem then? When just one person out of the consensus has the smart and right decision but it is discarded because the consensus says otherwise.

On companies there is a hierarchy, no matter how much we try to flatten it and how good our intentions of participation are. You can overcome a bad consensus relying on trust. If the team trust the leader, is more prepared to “let go” and override the consensus, why? Because they respect the leader, and they know it cares about them. Something like... _“I would prefer to go another direction, but I have been wrong in the past. On the other hand, I trust the leader because he/she always deliver and sticks to his/her word”_. This trust relationship favors the arrangement “I let you get your way, because some other times I get my way”

# Reflections of past managers

We all have managers that we aspire to be like. These are a few of the best features that I have been experiencing along the way:

- Leader that gives you time and space to learn how to solve a problem, research the proper technologies and needs to solve the problem.
- When leaders have the eye to recognize and praise work that it is not easy or obvious to see.
- The leader takes a chance on you letting you grow and learn.

But success it's not an ideal place to start if you want are looking for a way to learn, it is easier to learn from mistakes. Let's explore some leadership mistakes.

# Reflections of past managers mistakes

Everyone remembers the past mistakes of previous bosses. Therefore, as a leader, it’s important to remember that if one makes a thoughtless comment or if one reacts emotionally, it’s easy to pretend that will be forgotten by tomorrow, yet your team members will more likely remember this moment for a very long time.

## Micromanaging

The need to control every single task that the team performs. This has a lot of problems:

The team is only as good as the manager is. There is a funnel for every single thing that has to be done. It does not matter the talents of each team member individually because everything gets tainted by the manager filter. So, in a way, the manager has "more hands" but not "more minds". A really powerful team accomplishes great things by working together, combining intellects. If some manager is telling everyone what to do, there is no mind anymore, no problem-solving. Just a bunch of extra hands for the manager to do all the job.

There is no way to establish trust. As I mentioned before, trust is a two-way street. Micromanaging sends the implicit message that the leader does not trust the team. That is why he or she needs to control and approve every single task. Without mutual trust, there is a glass ceiling extremely low to the ground, and you are never going to be able to top that limit.

## Fragile ego

I was at a company once where my manager had a fragile ego. I can’t think of any other personality trait more hurtful for the team well-being. I remember a situation that will illustrate the point. These are the facts:

There were two teams working together, team A and team B, both teams work on the same project. Team A leader (let's say, his name is Snoopy) and team B leader (let’s say his name is Garfield) agreed between them on some strategy for dealing with third party integrations. On a regular catch-up meeting, both teams put things together and communicate updates and potential blockers between the two teams. On one of those regular meetings, Snoopy was supposed to communicate the agreement about the third parties. He did, but it was not the agreement that he made with Garfield, it was another option. Garfield leader goes ballistic and mad. He sustains that he did not agree to this new arrangement, that this new situation harms his team, and it's not in their best interest. Garfield team members don't understand anything, by the way, they are just looking at the situation uncomfortably.

Garfield leader throws a tantrum in front of everyone because he feels the need to "defend his team", to “put him in his place” and show him “who's boss”. His ego is fragile, so fragile that a simple last-minute change of plans threatens his greatness, so he must act out.

The final consequences are... the problem is still there; everyone is looking to each other weirdly and the relations between team Snoopy and team Garfield are seriously damaged. By consequence, the two teams will not work together as well as they did minutes ago. Who knows how much time it will take to restore the relationship, how much money will be lost because the teams are not going to deliver as always.

Beware of managers that use formulas like “I will defend my team no matter what” or any other moral boosting, school yard, childish catchphrase. This is not a war, we don't need epic, we don't need a father figure, we need clear leadership aligned with clear goals. Garfield has not the best interest of the team in mind but **his own interest** instead. He is more busy trying to look "powerful" in front of his team than to actually helping the team.

**What would be a better approach in a situation like that?**

The best interest of Garfield is to have a good relationship with Snoopy and vice-versa, for the sake of the project. So, both team leaders must establish a good relationship and try to align their agendas as much as possible in a way that does not hurt any of the two teams or the project.

If Garfield really thinks that this change of course harms his team, a better approach would have been just saying _"ok, we had a previous agreement and that has changed for some reason. Obviously, there is some new information that I don't have for this change to take place. Let's put a pin on that and discuss it off-line, share what the new information is, what are the new circumstances and let's try to find a solution that satisfy the interest of both teams."_

There, is this that difficult? Maybe Snoopy leader **has a good reason** for the change of plans, maybe there is something else, and we don't know the whole picture yet.

When your manager throws a tantrum in front of lots of people, rest assure, it's not defending your interests but his/her own.

Apologies to Snoopy and Garfield, the most beloved pets in the comic world.

## Not wanted to see reality

Another common mistake is to deny the reality that the team members are communicating and fall into the magical thinking that somehow, things are going to be ok.

I was working on a project a long time ago where all the senior engineers were constantly telling to management... "we can't continue like this", "if we don't fix the situation, the application will crash", "please, allocate some resources so we can save the situation"... guess what happened? Of course, the application crashed costing an enormous amount of money to recover it.

A good leader should listen to the team and have the boots on the ground. Not engage with fantasies and see the reality of the day to day of the project.

# When things go south

One of the not-so-nice part of the job is being able to deal with a disrupting team member. Normally people do their job well, but sometimes they don't like the direction of the team or the company. This creates a problem for the team performance and if it's not correctly address can make the team really dysfunctional.

First you should be able to agree with these disruptive members when they are right, your job is supporting the team, that also means those members. Your goal should be to figure out what is bothering them and try to fix it.

You want to support the team, of course, but you are also representing the company. No team member can go over the best interest of the company so you should try to find out the cause of the disagreement:

- Is this a misunderstanding of the problem? If so, the disrupting team member should propose different ideas
- Is this a misunderstanding of the vision, in which case the priority of requirements/use-cases could be different
- Or, maybe the problem & requirements are well understood yet the disagreement is based on "gut feel": where two people have different feeling on which way to go. Which means there’s not enough evidence to corroborate/validate the instincts of a team member

In business, we often don't have time to measure all outcomes before we make a choice. Therefore, the business decision is a gamble, there will a risk that you made the wrong choice.

But so, better to fail-fast (always measuring the solution as it's being developed), and change direction once you have enough evidence to justify the change. At the end of the day, the leader has to make their own choice and deal with the possible consequences.

As a leader, you should try to be transparent on the risks and thought process on how you came to a decision.

Yet, still, some team members won’t be happy with that, because they naturally think their instinct is the right choice. If you’re rational and transparent, it becomes their problem. You’ve done the best you can.

Remember too, that some people are naturally more disagreeable than others. Some people will disagree with you because it wasn't their idea. You can't persuade all the people all the time. Knowing about the different personality types will help contextualize how/why people act the way they do

A good staring point could be _<a href="https://www.simplypsychology.org/big-five-personality.html" target="_blank" alt="The Big Five Personality Traits">the Big Five Personality Traits</a>_, heavily used by psychologists and counsellors. But also, this model has its criticisms. No model is perfect, but it's a starting point.

If despite all your efforts, there is still some rogue team member, you should document its behavior. Write up some notes and minutes when discussing the different points of views, begin always super polite, unemotional and neutral:

_"This is what I think, this is what you think, this is where we agreed on, this is where we disagree on. Thank you for your opinion, thank you for the discussion and your feedback."_

Avoid to be emotional when trying to resolve a conflict like that. When things got emotional that is when the communication gets disrupted: If you are emotional, you are probably not listening.

# The not so happy ending

Most of the time, you make it up as you go...

Every day in the job there are new challenges, so you cannot train in advance for every problem, you must trust your instincts and experience, and so you do _“make it up as you go along”._ The big secret is: everyone else is doing it too. Learning to manage your anxiety and stress in new or uncertain situations is important. Staying calm during uncertain times help you keep your eyes open, so you can be proactive rather than only reactive to changes. If you feel that you’re freaking out then treat that as a marker, a sign, that it’s time to take a step back. Sleep on it, have a good breakfast, then think about how the problem looks. Following the principles and all you have learnt. It’s ok to make mistakes (it’s better still if you notice when you make them) .
